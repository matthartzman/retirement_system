Plan Status

Open or create your plan, see what is still missing, and pick what to do next.

What it is for

This is the home base. It shows your setup progress, report readiness (what is still missing before you can build), and the next step. Start with opening or creating a plan. Once a plan is loaded, save changes, build reports, and review results from here. Start New and Open Current are the two starting points, and they are mutually exclusive.

What it connects to

Starting new clears all household-specific data (entered client facts, income, spending, and asset values) but keeps system defaults (inflation, return, and tax assumptions). Opening the current plan reloads everything previously saved: income, assets, Roth strategy, Social Security, account balances, and year-to-date (YTD) transactions.

Your options

Start New when building a first draft or testing a completely different household. Open Current to continue where you left off. Bulk import and export utilities are in Data & Backups (the System Configuration Console, under Settings).

What it changes

Starting new on top of existing work permanently clears the saved data unless you have exported a backup first, so if unsure, export a backup before starting new.

Words used here

  • Readiness (readiness checks)

Household & People

Who is in the household, when each person was born and plans to retire, and where you live and file taxes.

What it is for

Enter each person's legal name, date of birth, retirement date, and mortality/planning-horizon age, plus the household's filing status, state of residence, and the survivor income and filing assumptions. This page defines the people, tax identity, state, and filing status used by the model, plus when earned income stops, when retirement spending begins, and how long the household should be projected. These are identity and tax-foundation inputs rather than strategy levers. Mortality age is the planning horizon, meaning how long the plan should last, not a prediction of how long you will live.

What it connects to

Birth dates set ages. Ages connect to retirement timing, Social Security, Medicare, required distributions (RMDs), mortality horizon, survivor years, and estate planning. Filing status and state connect to tax brackets, state taxes, and survivor tax treatment. Filing status sets the brackets across the entire projection. Retirement dates connect to work income, savings contributions, healthcare bridge years, withdrawals, Roth conversion room, and Monte Carlo sequence risk. Life expectancy and survivor assumptions determine late-life taxes, RMD pressure, and legacy values. Survivor assumptions drive the Survivor stress report and also appear there for review before a rebuild.

Your options

Use actual dates when possible. Use MFJ (married filing jointly) for a married couple, Single after a survivor period when appropriate, HOH (head of household) only when its rules truly apply, and the resident state expected for the modeled period. Earlier retirement increases years funded by assets. Later retirement adds income, reduces withdrawals, and shortens the funding horizon.

What it changes

Changing ages or filing status can materially change lifetime taxes, RMD timing, Social Security timing, Medicare years, survivor cash flow, and terminal net worth. Changing state may affect state tax, estate tax, and residency optimizer output. Retiring later usually increases terminal net worth and probability of success. Retiring earlier usually lowers liquidity and may reduce success unless spending, income, or home-equity assumptions compensate.

Words used here

  • Filing status
  • Planning horizon
  • Mortality age
  • Survivor assumptions
  • Tax bracket

Work Income

Pay you still earn and what you save from it until you retire.

What it is for

This page captures earned income (salary or self-employment), payroll assumptions, employer benefits, and savings including retirement plan contributions, while the household is still working.

What it connects to

Earned income feeds cash flow, payroll taxes, retirement-plan contributions, taxable income, Affordable Care Act (ACA) and IRMAA-sensitive income in transition years, and the year-to-date (YTD) income comparison. Business salary level affects payroll tax and business-income deductions. Contribution amounts add to account balances annually until the retirement date, and connect to future withdrawal sources.

Your options

Salary/W-2 values are gross annual amounts. Self-employment or S-Corp fields should match the intended entity strategy. Contribution fields should reflect annual expected savings, not current account balances.

What it changes

Higher earned income generally improves near-term cash flow and the terminal net worth, but can increase lifetime taxes and reduce Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidy eligibility. Higher contributions usually improve terminal net worth but reduce spendable current cash. High earned income in late working years compresses Roth conversion room below the bracket ceiling — coordinate with the Roth Conversion page when the retirement date is near.

Words used here

  • Payroll assumptions
  • Retirement plan contributions
  • Business-income deductions
  • Roth conversion room
  • Bracket ceiling

Social Security & Pensions

Social Security, pension and annuity income: how much, when it starts, and what continues to a surviving spouse.

What it is for

This page captures Social Security (claiming age and benefit for each person), pension amounts, annuity income and their start ages, and other retirement income streams. These are income floors that can reduce portfolio withdrawals.

What it connects to

Claim ages, survivor percentages, COLA settings, and present-value horizons connect to cash flow, tax brackets, survivor results, and allocation coverage. Survivor percentages control how much pension or annuity income continues to the survivor after the first death. Pensions and annuities with survivor protection can be treated as fixed-income-equivalent coverage in the allocation analysis (fixed-income-like coverage); set the coverage option in the Asset allocation & location section of Optimize.

Your options

Enter each person’s Social Security benefit from their Social Security statement when available, along with the planned claiming age and the household spousal/survivor policy. COLA choices indicate whether the payment keeps pace with inflation. Delaying past full retirement age adds about 8% per year up to age 70; the higher earner’s delay has the greatest survivor income impact.

What it changes

Higher guaranteed income usually improves probability of success and reduces withdrawals, but may increase taxable income and affect Roth conversion room. Delayed claiming may reduce early liquidity but raise later survivor-protected income. Early claiming opens gap years for Roth conversion if other income is low — model the joint timing strategy on the Roth Conversion page.

Words used here

  • Claiming age
  • Full retirement age
  • Spousal / survivor policy
  • Cost-of-living settings (COLA)
  • Fixed-income-equivalent coverage

Spending Model

Everything you spend and earn, sorted into categories.

What it is for

The category manager and model for all of your income and spending: review the Tracking Type → Group → Category model and manage the category tree. It should account for all income and all expenses, including taxes, except internal transfers. Budget references and projection controls live here, one editable section per Tracking Type: Core Expenses, Housing, Wellness, Travel, Large Discretionary, Taxes, and Business. Current-year transactions and spending analysis are on Actual Spending, under Track & Reconcile.

What it connects to

Core spending flows into annual withdrawals, taxable income, liquidity stress and Monte Carlo success; growth mode and freeze year set how it changes over time. Spending categories control how actual transactions roll up. Housing costs, a planned home sale and next-step housing change annual cash flow and net worth. Large discretionary rows add cash needs in specific years and connect to Optimize because they are often adjustable. Travel budgets are edited here and shown read-only elsewhere.

Your options

Use CPI/general inflation when spending should rise with inflation, a manual override when it should grow at another rate, and the freeze year when increases stop. Map categories in the Spending Categories hierarchy; use Advanced Auto-Mapping Rules only for merchant text that should repeat automatically. Enter housing costs as annual amounts, and mark each large item as must-fund, flexible or aspirational. Each section's help button has the detail.

What it changes

Higher recurring spending usually lowers terminal net worth and probability of success; lower spending or an earlier freeze usually improves both, especially in the first retirement decade. Selling and downsizing can unlock home equity, while renting reduces balance-sheet exposure but may raise long-term housing costs. Large discretionary items that fall early can sharply reduce success, so delaying them or making them conditional helps. Separating Travel prevents double counting.

Words used here

  • Tracking Type
  • Group (spending)
  • Category
  • Internal transfers
  • Auto-Mapping Rules
  • Terminal net worth
  • Probability of success

Investment Holdings

Each investment you own, one row per purchase, and the activity behind it.

What it is for

One row per tax lot: each purchase or reinvestment of a security, with account, ticker, shares, price, cost basis, and purchase date. Add holdings from your broker here — a broker statement or CSV export usually lists everything a row needs directly.

What it connects to

Holdings drive account totals, allocation drift, pricing, tax-lot sell guidance, rebalancing recommendations, and year-to-date growth for investment accounts. Account names must match names used on Withdrawal Sequencing and Asset Allocation. Lot-level cost basis enables tax-aware sell guidance in the allocation output.

Your options

Use CASH at price 1.00 for money market and cash positions. Keep separate lots per purchase when tax-basis guidance matters — blended basis reduces sell-guidance precision. For large broker downloads, export a backup before importing to replace all.

What it changes

More complete cost basis and purchase dates improve taxable sell guidance and estimated tax impact on trade recommendations.

Words used here

  • Tax lot
  • Cost basis
  • Ticker
  • Tax-aware sell guidance

Cash Reserves

How much cash to keep on the side before the plan sells investments.

What it is for

This page captures liquidity reserve rules: the cash reserve floor the plan protects before drawing investments. Home value and mortgage balance are on Other Assets & Debts; home sale is in Optimize → Next Housing Move.

What it connects to

Reserve rules connect to withdrawal sequencing, liquidity floors, and Monte Carlo failure modes. The reserve floor is the last buffer in probability analysis — the plan counts as failing when it cannot maintain this floor without depleting all accounts.

Your options

Reserve rules set how many years of spending to hold outside the investment portfolio, and which account bucket should be protected.

What it changes

Higher cash reserves improve liquidity resilience but can reduce expected return if too much capital stays out of the portfolio.

Words used here

  • Reserve floor
  • Probability analysis

Other Assets & Debts

Things you own or owe that are not in your investment accounts, such as HSA, 529 and a note owed to you.

What it is for

This page captures non-portfolio assets such as notes receivable, HSA, DAF, 529 plans, equity compensation, collectibles, vehicles, personal property, and policy-related assets.

What it connects to

Asset type controls where the value appears, whether it is liquid, how it is taxed, and whether it affects estate, education, Wellness, charitable, or growth modules. Planned sale dates connect non-liquid assets to future cash flow. Donor-advised fund configuration is set on Charitable Giving.

Your options

Pick the asset type that best describes each item's economic purpose, then enter today's fair value, timing assumptions (for anything illiquid, a planned sale date), and whether the asset is liquid or restricted. Notes receivable should use principal and schedule fields, not straight-line extrapolation. HSA balances grow tax-free and should reflect intended use.

What it changes

Higher liquid asset values usually improve success probability. Higher illiquid asset values increase terminal net worth and estate values but may not fund spending unless sale or borrowing is modeled.

Words used here

  • Note receivable
  • HSA (Health Savings Account)
  • 529 plan
  • Equity compensation
  • Illiquid
  • Donor-advised fund (DAF)

Home Equity Line

Borrow against your home for big purchases instead of selling investments early.

What it is for

Model a home equity line of credit (HELOC) that bridges large discretionary spending in early retirement years with home equity, instead of drawing from liquid assets, keeping invested assets untouched.

What it connects to

HELOC draw reduces gap filled by liquid assets, allowing taxable/IRA balances to compound longer. Interest is paid from cash flow annually. The projection automatically draws from the line, up to available credit, when large discretionary spending creates a cash gap, then repays the outstanding balance from home sale proceeds.

Your options

Enter a credit limit, the last draw year (the last year of the draw period), an initial interest rate, and an annual rate drift.

What it changes

HELOC improves terminal net worth when the compound benefit of undisturbed liquid assets exceeds borrowing costs. It worsens outcomes when interest drag or reduced home equity at sale outweigh the investment benefit.

Words used here

  • Credit limit
  • Draw year
  • Rate drift
  • Discretionary spending
  • Home sale proceeds

Rental Properties

Income, costs and eventual sale of each rental property.

What it is for

One record per rental property: add each rental property, or convert your own home to a rental. The summary here is an estimate from your entries; the build applies your real income.

What it connects to

Rent, operating expenses, mortgage interest and depreciation net on Schedule E into AGI; passive-loss limits, NIIT, state tax, cash flow, net worth and the sale (with depreciation recapture) follow. Rental income is taxed on Schedule E: rent less expenses, interest and depreciation.

Your options

Fill the Standard fields for each property; Show advanced adds the Expert fields (placed-in-service month, capital improvements, per-year overrides). Enter a property once: here, not also in Other Assets. Turn the feature on in Plan Features.

What it changes

Rent above expenses adds taxable income; a loss is limited by the passive-activity rules and carried forward until you sell. Converting your home moves its carrying costs from spending to Schedule E.

Words used here

  • Depreciation
  • Passive-activity rules
  • Schedule E

Insurance

Your insurance policies and the yearly benefit values from each insurer's illustration.

What it is for

This page records year-by-year carrier illustration values (death benefits) for annuities, riders and special income, plus all insurance policies (life, disability, long-term care, umbrella, auto, home, property and casualty, and other), with the owner, insured, beneficiary, face amount, and premiums for each. It combines a year-by-year schedule with individual policy records — not a generic account balance table.

What it connects to

Each annuity policy row connects to survivor, estate, and legacy reporting; these totals appear in the Survivor and Estate report sections. Year columns show how protection changes over time and whether a benefit disappears before the end of the plan. Policy premiums connect to cash flow each year; benefit amounts appear in the Survivor and Long-Term Care Stress report sections.

Your options

Enter the carrier illustration values for each year for annuities, from each policy illustration; use 0 for years with no benefit (no death benefit available). For any insurance policy, use policy type, premium end, term end, benefit amount, and owner/insured fields consistently. Keep policy names consistent with other insurance/annuity entries; they must match any cross-references on stress pages.

What it changes

Higher death benefits improve survivor/legacy protection but may come with lower investment growth, liquidity restrictions, or ongoing rider costs that should be reflected elsewhere. More insurance coverage can reduce downside risk but premiums may lower terminal net worth if no claim occurs. Rider benefits that step down or expire early can leave the survivor without protection — compare benefit schedules against the planning horizon set on Household & People.

Words used here

  • Carrier illustration
  • Rider
  • Beneficiary
  • Face amount

Estate Inputs

What you plan to leave, to whom, and how, including gifts and charity.

What it is for

This page captures estate-tax, trust, beneficiary, legacy-planning, and gifting assumptions. It tells the model how assets should be interpreted after death or for survivor planning. Enter federal and state exemption elections, trust structure, beneficiary needs, lifetime gifting, and charitable intent for this household.

What it connects to

Federal/state exemptions, CST/QTIP settings, beneficiary needs, special-needs planning, gifting, and charitable intent connect to legacy value, estate-tax exposure, survivor analysis, Roth strategy scoring, and executor-oriented workbook notes. Insurance policies (life, disability, long-term care, umbrella, and property and casualty) are entered on Insurance. Long-term-care hybrid policies with an investment component should also appear on Other Assets & Debts.

Your options

Use monitor/balanced/strong estate objectives depending on whether estate tax is a watch item or a decision driver. Enter trust and beneficiary facts only when they are part of the intended plan. The federal exemption can change with law updates — confirm the current-law amount in Settings and model the impact of any reduction in Scenarios.

What it changes

More aggressive estate-tax planning may reduce projected estate tax and improve legacy quality, but can reduce flexibility if assets are transferred, restricted, or earmarked too early.

Words used here

  • Federal / state exemption (estate)
  • Trust structure
  • Lifetime gifting

Roth Conversion

Decide how much pre-tax money to move into a Roth account each year, and how to weigh the trade-offs.

What it is for

This page sets how voluntary Roth conversions are sized and scored: the conversion policy, the ceiling (bracket or fixed dollar), Medicare income surcharge guardrails, and objective weights for tax, legacy, survivor, and estate. It is a planning optimizer, not a record of taxes already paid. Forced conversion rows are separate: they represent actions already taken or imposed for a scenario, and run before the optimizer, reducing the space available for voluntary conversions.

What it connects to

The Roth objective, tax-bracket ceiling, IRMAA guardrail, legacy weight, estate-tax weight, and survivor-risk weight work together. Tight guardrails preserve near-term liquidity and Medicare premium control; loose guardrails allow larger conversions when long-term tax savings or legacy value justify the current tax cost.

Your options

Choose the policy first — the page shows only controls relevant to that policy. Bracket-fill policies convert up to a tax bracket, a marginal rate ceiling determined by the filing status on Household & People. Medicare income surcharge (IRMAA) guardrails stop or warn near Medicare premium cliffs, preventing projected income from crossing premium tiers during conversion years. Fixed-dollar options force a chosen annual amount. Objective weights tell the scorer whether taxes, terminal net worth, survivor outcomes, or legacy value matter most.

What it changes

Higher conversion amounts usually increase current-year taxes and may reduce liquid assets, but can lower future RMDs, survivor tax compression, and lifetime taxes. More conservative guardrails usually protect near-term cash flow and Medicare premiums, but may leave future tax savings unused.

Words used here

  • Roth conversion
  • Conversion policy
  • Bracket ceiling
  • Medicare income surcharge
  • Premium tiers
  • Objective weights
  • Marginal rate

Charitable Giving

How you give to charity: direct gifts, a donor-advised fund, or gifts straight from an IRA.

What it is for

This strategy page covers the charitable giving vehicle: cash (direct gift), donor-advised fund, or qualified charitable distribution. Annual giving amounts are entered on Core spending. Business-entity choice (S-Corp vs limited liability company (LLC)) is entered on Work Income.

What it connects to

Charitable vehicle choice connects to deductions, lifetime taxes, and legacy, and appears in a dedicated workbook sheet.

Your options

Choose how charitable gifts are funded. Donor-advised funds are most effective when contributed in a high-income year and granted over time. Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs) are available at age 70½ and satisfy required distributions tax-free; use them after RMD age where appropriate.

What it changes

Qualified charitable distributions also reduce adjusted gross income, which can lower income-related Medicare surcharge tiers — model in combination with Roth Conversion. Charitable vehicle choice can lower taxes and increase legacy, but may reduce near-term liquidity.

Words used here

  • Donor-advised fund (DAF)
  • Qualified charitable distribution (QCD)
  • Adjusted gross income (AGI)
  • Required distributions

Account Tax Basis

The inputs the cash pair-status tax needs for Roth and HSA accounts, and its on/off switch.

What it is for

Collects the inputs for taxing cash you report withdrawn from an account: your own Roth contributions and first-contribution year, the HSA expense bank, and the Cash Pair Status Tax switch. Pre-tax accounts need no inputs. Taxable accounts are taxed through the Investment Sales Ledger, so there is nothing to enter for them.

What it connects to

Only the first plan year is affected, and only when Cash Pair Status Tax is on (off by default). The forced draw replaces planned draws and is taxed as ordinary income.

Your options

Roth: enter total own contributions (not conversions or earnings) and the tax year of the first contribution to any Roth IRA. HSA: enter the qualified expenses still available; blank treats every withdrawal as qualified. Leave a field blank if you do not know it.

What it changes

The 10% early-withdrawal penalty on Roth earnings and the 20% pre-65 HSA penalty are not modelled. Review the worked example before turning the switch on.

Words used here

  • Contribution basis
  • 5-year rule
  • Qualified expenses

529 & Equity Compensation

College savings plans and stock awards from an employer.

What it is for

This page collects the 529 & Equity Compensation plan inputs: 529 education savings sections and equity compensation grants. The same rows are editable on Other Assets & Debts; business entity choice (S-Corp vs limited liability company (LLC)) is on Work Income.

What it connects to

529 balances and contributions connect to education spending and legacy. Equity compensation connects to earned income, payroll and capital-gains tax, and the projection reads it directly when its module is on. Each feature here is an optional module. A feature that is off keeps everything you have entered and says so on its own section, so turning one off never removes your data.

Your options

Enter one 529 section per beneficiary or goal, and the grant, vesting and exercise assumptions for any equity compensation; use grant-level assumptions rather than a single blended figure where the vesting years differ.

What it changes

Equity compensation can concentrate both portfolio risk and taxable income in a few years — check the lifetime-tax and allocation results after entering it.

Words used here

  • 529 plan
  • Vesting
  • Exercise
  • Optional module

Optimize

One place to adjust the main levers: Roth conversions, investment mix, giving and home-equity borrowing.

What it is for

The decision hub for the main levers: Roth conversions, asset allocation and location, charitable giving, and home-equity borrowing. It shows your current projected outcome and screens the practical changes most likely to move terminal net worth or probability of success. Open a section to work on one lever at a time; each is the same input page it always was. Decide the strategy here, then test it on Stress Test and compare alternatives on Scenarios. Sections whose optional module is off show how to enable it.

What it connects to

Each lever estimates its own effect from the latest build and your current plan inputs. Spending, retirement timing, reserve, home-equity, Roth, tax and risk levers point back to the source pages where the real values are changed. Allocation choices connect to holdings and trade guidance, and withdrawal order connects to taxable income, required minimum distributions and Roth preservation. HSA withdrawal timing is controlled on Other Assets & Debts. Each section's help button explains its own inputs.

Your options

Change one test amount at a time to isolate its likely effect. Rank by terminal net worth when legacy value is the goal, and by probability of success when reliable funding of spending is the goal. Each section's help covers its own choices: target state, allocation mode, include and exclude rules, and withdrawal order.

What it changes

Levers that improve both terminal net worth and probability of success are the strongest. Some trade one against the other: higher risk may raise net worth but lower success, larger reserves may raise success but lower return, and Roth conversions may lower lifetime taxes but reduce near-term liquidity. Rebuild and compare after each change, because a change can improve one measure while worsening another.

Words used here

  • Asset allocation and location
  • Lever
  • Terminal net worth
  • Probability of success
  • Allocation sleeve

Stress Test

See how the plan holds up if things go badly: poor markets, early death, long-term care, divorce.

What it is for

Adverse tests, not forecasts: Monte Carlo probability of success, the early death of one spouse, long-term-care cost, and, only when that module is on, divorce. This page controls how uncertainty is simulated across markets, inflation, tax indexing and wellness-cost shocks, and how probability of success is measured. Divorce is not for ordinary married or survivor planning.

What it connects to

Each section is an optional workbook module, so it has inputs only once its module is on in Plan Features; the page is hidden when all four are off. Simulation settings connect to probability of success, downside wealth, failure timing and build time. Survivor assumptions connect to filing status, required minimum distributions and late-life net worth (the full result is the Survivor sheet). Long-term-care cost and coverage connect to late-life spending and liquidity. Divorce assumptions connect to taxes, ownership and cash flow.

Your options

Set the inputs for the tests that apply, then rebuild to see them in the workbook. Quick mode is faster and approximate; exact mode is slower but advisor-ready; more trials reduce random noise. Review the survivor assumptions (also on Household & People) and the long-term-care coverage before rebuilding. Turn the divorce module on only for an explicit scenario, using actual legal or negotiated assumptions, otherwise labelled hypothetical.

What it changes

More conservative settings usually lower probability of success but make it more reliable; faster settings suit drafts but not final recommendations. Early death often compresses the survivor's tax brackets and cuts household income, and insurance and Roth balances soften that. A long-term-care event is a major downside risk, so compare outcomes with and without coverage. Split-household divorce assumptions can materially reduce terminal net worth and raise lifetime taxes.

Words used here

  • Monte Carlo
  • Survivor stress
  • Long-term care
  • Probability of success

Scenarios

Save "what if" versions of your plan, such as retiring two years later.

What it is for

Change sets are named, deterministic what-if cases that you stage and save from any source page and compare side by side in the Workbench; each compares a specific bundle of lever/assumption changes against the base plan. It is a comparison mode, not a random-draw stress test like Monte Carlo. Rank individual levers and review saved cases there.

What it connects to

Scenario rows can change returns, inflation, home sale assumptions, spending, tax, or timing for workbook scenario sheets and risk narratives. Economy and home-sale stress cases belong here.

Your options

Use one scenario per clear question with a specific answer: retire later (for example two years later), sell the home (for example in 2028), inflation stress, low returns, spending cut, or tax change. Keep scenario labels descriptive so workbook comparisons are readable. Use Stress Test for probability ranges and adverse assumptions.

What it changes

Scenarios that lean adverse often reduce terminal net worth and success indicators, but comparing bundles side by side shows which lever combinations protect the plan best.

Words used here

  • Change set
  • Deterministic
  • Lever

Workbench

Compare your plan with the saved what-ifs and decide which to adopt.

What it is for

The unified planning flow for comparing the baseline, named change sets, scenario ideas, stress assumptions and stress-suite results in one place, then deciding what to adopt (final decisions). Planning cases are browser-local change sets (notes) that do not alter the saved plan.

What it connects to

The workbench connects Optimize (the strategy levers), Scenarios, Stress Test settings, and Build & Results around the same Baseline → Change Set → Run Type → Impact → Decision vocabulary. Saved cases help organize what you are testing without mutating the saved plan.

Your options

Pick a baseline, stage a change set to test (or save a staged edit or strategy as a case), choose Scenario or Stress as the run type, then review Impact and record a Decision: use the Decision panel to adopt, archive, or leave it as reference. Adopted changes do not alter the saved plan until you explicitly jump to source pages, edit inputs, save, and rebuild.

What it changes

A planning case can clarify what to test next, but it does not change workbook results by itself. Only saved source-page edits followed by a rebuild affect terminal net worth, lifetime taxes, Monte Carlo success, and report outputs.

Words used here

  • Baseline
  • Change set
  • Run type
  • Planning cases

Actual Spending

Compare what you really spent this year with what the plan assumed.

What it is for

Import and manage current-year income and expense transactions for the selected year, and see how they compare with the spending model: a current-year performance summary (spending, income, portfolio growth), a budget vs actuals tracker, and the link to the 30-year retirement model.

What it connects to

Transactions from Actual Spending feed both the summary and the tracker. Category assignment happens on Spending Model: Spending Categories controls how transactions are assigned to the canonical Tracking Type → Group → Category hierarchy. Accounts & Sources controls account/source type, prior-year balances, and current values.

Your options

Import and assign transactions for the selected year on the page for that year. Upload a CSV from your bank or brokerage. Use Replace all to start fresh or Add to merge new transactions with existing ones. Remove Duplicates deduplicates by Date + Merchant + Amount + Account. Required CSV columns: Date, Merchant, Category, Account, Amount. Optional: Original Statement, Notes, Tags, Owner. Only current-year rows are imported. The summary shows actual vs projected spending, income, and growth. The tracker compares annualized actuals to category budgets and to the retirement model core spending assumption. Compare annualized spending with the plan on Analysis, then use Sync Actual Rate to update the 30-year model before building.

What it changes

Spending rate above the model assumption reduces projected net worth. Identifying over-budget groups early — before the annual rebuild — gives time to recalibrate Core spending or adjust plans. More complete transaction data improves the accuracy of the year-to-date (YTD) spending, income, and growth analysis on Actual Spending.

Words used here

  • Annualized
  • Sync actual rate
  • Accounts & Sources

Checking & Savings

Your checking and savings accounts: a balance and the date it was last updated.

What it is for

Lists each cash account with its balance and last-updated date. Add an account with a name, a balance and a date, or delete one. Link an account to the account import and the import keeps its balance and date current.

What it connects to

These balances count as cash in the plan, the same as before. Accounts link to the import by name or by the pick-list when you add one; Account Reconciliation shows every difference and can undo an update.

Your options

Add, delete, or link to an imported account. A balance that comes from the import is not edited here: change it through Account Reconciliation.

What it changes

Cash balances change net worth, the spending reserve and the starting values the next build uses.

Words used here

  • Last updated
  • Account import

Account Reconciliation

Check the plan's account balances against your imported daily balances and fix differences.

What it is for

Compares each account number in the app with the balance the import reports (the imported daily balances), as of a date you pick, lists what needs a decision, and offers a way to copy the imported number into the app.

What it connects to

Accounts match by name, by a match you save, or by importing your mapping CSV. Each match has a policy: ask me, always update (copied at the nightly import unless the change is large or a plan-start input), app is source, or ignore. Investments are never changed here: investment accounts are valued from holdings, so they are report only.

Your options

Each difference needs a choice: update the app, ignore it this time, always update from the import, or keep the app as the source. Every change is logged and can be undone: use Undo in the change log, or Undo all automatic updates; it refuses if the value was edited since. Update app previews the old and new value before anything is written.

What it changes

A corrected balance changes net worth, year-to-date (YTD) growth and the starting values the next build uses. Plan inputs (home, autos, mortgage) are plan-start figures; rebuild after changing or applying them.

Words used here

  • Reconciliation
  • Report only
  • Plan-start figures

Sales Ledger

Record investment sales you have made, fix their lots, and see the gain on each.

What it is for

Keeps a running record of realized sales, one row per lot sold: sale date, account, symbol, shares, proceeds, fees, the lot's cost basis and purchase date. It works out the gain and whether it is short or long term. The page has four tabs: Fix lots & transactions (sales waiting for lots and the transaction log), Sales ledger (the sales themselves), Tax year (gains, wash sales and carryforward) and Import (broker files, 1099-B and CSV).

What it connects to

The Needs attention strip at the top links straight to anything that wants a look, and a small badge on a tab shows where it is. Each section can be opened or closed, and the page remembers your choice; a section with something new to look at opens itself. The tax-year panel nets the year's sales and tracks your capital-loss carryforward and possible wash sales. The year-end checklist is in Year-End Transition. Nothing else in the plan reads the ledger yet: entering a sale here does not change your projection.

Your options

Add a sale by hand, or paste or choose a CSV and preview it first: duplicates are skipped and any bad line stops the whole file. Void a sale to take it out of the totals; Restore brings it back. Enter last year's short- and long-term loss carryforward in the tax-year panel, and use Check for wash sales to flag losses with a purchase of the same security within 30 days. Expand all and Collapse all open or close every section on a tab, and Density switches between roomy and compact rows on a computer.

What it changes

None on the projection yet. Gain is proceeds minus fees minus basis, blank for pre-tax, Roth and HSA accounts. A sale is long term only when it is after the first anniversary of the purchase.

Words used here

  • Cost basis

Year-End Transition

Work through the new-year checklist: start the new year's tracking and close last year's cash reconciliation.

What it is for

Lists the steps for moving from one year to the next, in order: clear any overdue reference-constant items, start the new year's account tracking, update the personal numbers that changed, and close last year's cash reconciliation. Each step shows Done, Open or Not applicable from your data and links to the page that does it. Plan to do it in the first week of January.

What it connects to

Starting tracking is on Actual Spending, Accounts & Sources: it copies each account's current value into Prior Year End Balance, dated 12/31 of the prior year. Closing the cash year is on the Sales Ledger, under Cash in investment accounts, after you import the final broker files and resolve any unmatched rows. Updating personal numbers is a confirmation you make here.

Your options

Follow Go to this step to open the page that does each step. Use the confirmation button on a step once you have reviewed it, and Undo confirmation if you need to reopen it. To close the cash year, click Close for the year that just ended; build a report in December so a saved run that projected that year exists. A late broker file shows Changed since close, and Reopen lets you close again. Nothing closes a year automatically.

What it changes

None on the projection. Closing the cash year freezes that year's reported withdrawals and the plan's figure for it, and feeds no tax number.

Words used here

  • Reconciliation

Build & Results

Check the plan is ready, build the reports, then review and download them.

What it is for

One workspace for output: report readiness, build, impact, results, downloads and a read-only review of everything entered in the plan. Readiness is a pre-build checklist that adds no new calculations. Impact shows what changed in the latest build compared with the session baseline; it is a review and revert tool, not a data-entry page.

What it connects to

Risk tolerance, spending flexibility, longevity, tax strategy, Roth objectives, inflation, returns, estate intent and liquidity assumptions each connect to several workbook sections. Save Changes stores entered values; Download Workbook saves, builds and delivers in one step when there are unsaved changes or no current build. The impact comparison uses values captured before this editing session and values after the last successful build. The headline cards report lifetime consumption-and-transfer value (LCV), the present value of future taxes and worst-case ending wealth.

Your options

Build current reports (the readiness checks sit just above the Build button), review impact and results, then download or print the package; resolve required-field warnings first. Use Save Changes to save without rebuilding. Revert restores the before-values for edited inputs, and a rebuild shows whether that changes the output. Bulk import and export are in Data & Backups, under Settings.

What it changes

A successful build refreshes projected net worth, lifetime taxes, Monte Carlo success, allocation recommendations and every narrative section; the downloaded file reflects the last successful build, so download again after each rebuild. A positive change in terminal net worth is not automatically better if it raises lifetime taxes, liquidity stress or survivor risk. If a field looks wrong, correct it on the input page that holds it.

Results

What it is for

In-app view of every workbook sheet, chart and data table after a build — Cash Flow Projection, Monte Carlo, Allocation, Lifetime Taxes, Estate, and all strategy comparisons. Each sheet matches the same-named sheet in the Excel workbook.

What it connects to

Your options

The Cash Flow Projection, Monte Carlo, and Allocation sheets have the most complex rendering. If a chart or table looks incomplete in this view, the downloaded workbook is authoritative.

Economic & Tax Assumptions

Growth, inflation and tax-rule assumptions used for every year of the plan.

What it is for

This System page holds the default baseline return rates, inflation, medical cost escalation, COLA, tax bracket indexing, payroll, and Wellness assumptions, applied system-wide across all projections. Changes here affect every projection year simultaneously.

What it connects to

Inflation connects spending, Social Security COLA, tax brackets, IRMAA thresholds, Wellness costs, and capital-market projections. Tax constants and indexing assumptions connect to Roth and lifetime-tax analysis.

Your options

Use current-law/default assumptions for base plans; use Scenarios to compare alternative assumption sets and test alternatives without altering the base assumptions. Keep tax data source years current before relying on tax-sensitive recommendations. Return assumptions should reflect long-term expected rates, not recent performance.

What it changes

Medical inflation is the most sensitive late-life input — a 1% change compounds across 30 years and materially shifts Medicare and care costs. Higher inflation usually lowers real purchasing power and can reduce success unless income/assets adjust. Higher Wellness inflation raises late-life spending. Changing tax assumptions can materially change lifetime taxes and Roth recommendations.

Words used here

  • Medical inflation
  • Tax bracket indexing
  • Cost-of-living settings (COLA)

Plan Features

Turn optional parts of the plan on or off. Turning one off never deletes your entries.

What it is for

Enables or disables entire planning sections — long-term care stress, divorce planning, home equity line, charitable giving, special needs, equity compensation, 529 education funding, and others — chosen for this plan. Features are grouped by the part of life they cover (taxes, housing, protection, risk, and the rest) and can be filtered by the kind of question they answer.

What it connects to

Features that add navigation steps (their own page in the left menu, such as Home Equity Line and Rental Properties) must be turned on here before that step appears; features that only add workbook output can be toggled without changing the navigation. A feature that is on but depends on another that is off says what it is leaving out.

Your options

Enable a feature before entering its detail elsewhere in the plan — its input page won't appear in navigation until it's turned on. Turn a feature off to exclude it from the build without deleting its saved data; the row then shows how many entries are being held.

What it changes

Turning a feature off removes its section from the workbook build entirely, not just from navigation. Turning one on can add new required fields to complete before the plan is build-ready.

Words used here

  • Navigation steps (nav steps)

Glossary

5-year rule
Roth earnings are tax-free only if the first Roth contribution was at least five tax years ago and you are 59½ or older.
529 plan
A savings account for education costs. Growth is tax-free when the money is used for school.
Account import
Balances and transactions another process drops in a folder; the app can copy them into your accounts.
Accounts & Sources
The list of your bank, credit card and investment accounts, and where the app gets each account's numbers (typed in or imported).
Adjusted gross income (AGI)
Your total income for the year after a few specific subtractions. Many tax rules and Medicare costs are based on this one number.
Allocation sleeve
One slice of your target investment mix, such as US stocks or core bonds. A sleeve can be filled by a fund you hold, or by another asset that plays the same role.
Annualized
Turned into a full-year amount. If you spent $2,000 in the first two months, the annualized figure is $12,000 (six times as much).
Asset allocation and location
Allocation is how your money is split among kinds of investments, such as stocks and bonds. Location is which account holds each kind (for example, a tax-free account or a regular one), because that changes how much tax you pay.
Auto-Mapping Rules
Rules you set once so the app files transactions in the right spending category by itself, such as "anything from Corner Grocery goes under Food."
Baseline
Your plan as it stands today. Other versions are compared against it.
Beneficiary
The person or group who receives money or property from you, for example from a life insurance policy or a will.
Bracket ceiling
The top of a tax bracket you choose not to go above. Staying under it keeps the tax rate on your extra income from rising. See Tax bracket.
Business-income deductions
Business costs you can subtract from business income before tax is figured, so only the profit is taxed.
Carrier illustration
A document from an insurance company (the "carrier") that shows year by year what a policy is expected to be worth or pay.
Category
One kind of spending or income, such as Groceries or Rent. Categories are grouped. See Group and Tracking Type.
Change set
A saved list of "what if" changes to your plan, such as "retire two years later." It lets you try an idea without changing your real plan.
Claiming age
The age at which you start taking Social Security. Starting later gives a larger monthly check. Starting earlier gives a smaller one.
Column groups
Sets of related table columns that you can fold up or open so you see only what you need.
Conditional formatting
Spreadsheet colors or marks that change automatically based on the numbers, such as red for a negative amount.
Contribution basis
The money you put into a Roth account yourself, not conversions or earnings. It can come out first, tax-free.
Conversion policy
The rule you choose for how much to move into a Roth account each year. See Roth conversion.
Cost basis
What you originally paid for an investment, including fees. When you sell, you generally pay tax on the gain: the sale price minus the cost basis.
Cost-of-living settings (COLA)
How much a payment is expected to rise each year to keep up with higher prices. COLA stands for cost-of-living adjustment.
Credit limit
The most you are allowed to borrow on a credit line.
Depreciation
A yearly tax deduction that spreads the cost of a building or equipment over many years, because it wears out over time.
Deterministic
Gives the same answer every time you run it, because it uses fixed assumptions and no chance. The opposite of Monte Carlo.
Discretionary spending
Spending you could skip or change, such as travel or dining out. The opposite of must-pay bills.
Donor-advised fund (DAF)
A charity-run account where you put money now, get the tax benefit now, and choose which charities receive gifts from it later.
Draw year
A year in which you take money from a credit line. The "last draw year" is the final year you plan to borrow.
Equity compensation
Pay in the form of company stock or the right to buy it, on top of salary.
Exercise
Using a stock option to actually buy the company stock at the set price.
Face amount
The amount a life insurance policy pays out when the insured person dies.
Federal / state exemption (estate)
The amount you can leave to others before estate tax applies. Only the part above the exemption is taxed. Federal means the national government. State means your state's.
Filing status
The category you use on your tax return, such as single or married filing jointly. It decides which tax brackets and deductions apply.
Fixed-income-equivalent coverage
Counting steady, guaranteed payments (like a pension) as if they were bonds, because they pay a predictable amount. This lets the plan hold fewer bonds.
Full retirement age
The age when you get your full Social Security amount, with no cut for starting early and no bonus for waiting. For people born in 1960 or later it is 67.
Group (spending)
A set of related categories. For example, the Housing group might hold Rent, Utilities and Repairs.
Home sale proceeds
The cash left over when you sell your home, after paying off the mortgage and the costs of selling.
HSA (Health Savings Account)
A savings account for medical costs. Money goes in without income tax, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free when used for medical costs.
Illiquid
Hard to turn into cash quickly. A house or a collectible is illiquid. A savings account is not.
Impact
How much a change moves your results, such as how much longer your money lasts.
Internal transfers
Moving your own money between your own accounts, for example from checking to savings. It is not income and not spending.
Key performance indicator (KPI)
One of the headline numbers the app tracks to show how the plan is doing, such as terminal net worth or probability of success.
Last updated
The date the balance was last changed, by hand or by the account import.
Lever
One setting you can adjust to change the plan's outcome, such as the retirement date or how much you convert to a Roth.
Lifetime consumption-and-transfer value (LCV)
The total you spend over the plan, plus what is left to heirs after tax and estate tax. The app uses it as the headline score for comparing plans.
Lifetime gifting
Giving money or property to people while you are alive, rather than leaving it to them after your death.
Long-term care
Ongoing help with daily living, such as bathing and dressing, from a nursing home, assisted living or a home aide. It is often costly and is not covered by regular health insurance.
Marginal rate
The tax rate on your next dollar of income. It is not the average rate on all of your income.
Medical inflation
How fast health-care prices rise each year. It usually rises faster than ordinary prices.
Medicare income surcharge
An extra monthly charge added to Medicare premiums when your income is above set limits. Also called IRMAA.
Monte Carlo
A way to test your plan by running it thousands of times with randomly varied market results. It shows how often the plan works, not one single answer.
Mortality age
The age the plan assumes you will live to. The plan checks that your money lasts that long.
Navigation steps (nav steps)
The pages listed in the left-hand menu. Some appear only after you turn on the related feature.
Note receivable
Money someone else owes you under a written promise to repay, such as a loan you made.
Objective weights
Sliders that tell the app how much each goal matters, such as paying less tax versus leaving more to heirs, when it suggests a choice.
Optional module
A feature you can switch on or off. Off means it is left out of the plan. Your entries stay saved.
Overrides
Settings you choose yourself that replace the automatic ones.
Passive-activity rules
Tax rules that limit how much loss from something you do not work at (like a rental) can reduce your other income. Unused losses carry forward to later years.
Payroll assumptions
Assumptions about taxes and deductions taken from your paycheck, such as Social Security and Medicare taxes.
Plan-start figures
Balances and values as of the day the plan begins. They do not change on their own as time passes in the projection.
Planning cases
Saved "what if" versions of your plan kept in your browser. They do not change your real plan until you decide to.
Planning horizon
How many years the plan looks ahead, usually to a very old age so that you do not run out of money.
Premium tiers
Levels of Medicare cost. When your income crosses a limit, you move to a higher level and pay more each month.
Pricing snapshot
A saved copy of investment prices on one date.
Probability analysis
Shows the chance that your plan works out, for example "it works in 85 of 100 test runs."
Probability of success
The share of simulated futures in which your money lasts through the plan. It measures risk, not a promise: the app runs many random market paths to find it.
Qualified charitable distribution (QCD)
A gift sent straight from your IRA (a retirement account) to a charity, available from age 70½. It is not counted as taxable income.
Qualified expenses
Medical costs an HSA can pay tax-free. A withdrawal above your saved qualified expenses is taxed.
Rate drift
How an interest rate is expected to change over time rather than stay fixed.
Readiness (readiness checks)
Checks that your plan has the information it needs before you build reports. They list anything missing.
Reconciliation
Comparing two sets of numbers that should match, such as the app's balances and your bank's, and fixing differences.
Report only
Shown for information. You cannot change it here.
Required distributions
Money the law makes you take out of certain retirement accounts each year once you reach a set age, and usually pay tax on.
Reserve floor
The minimum amount of cash the plan keeps untouched as a safety cushion.
Retirement plan contributions
Money you put into a retirement account, such as a 401(k), from your pay.
Rider
An add-on to an insurance policy that adds or changes a benefit.
Roth conversion
Moving money from a regular retirement account into a Roth account. You pay income tax on the amount moved now. After that it can grow and be withdrawn tax-free later.
Roth conversion room
How much you can convert before reaching the top of your chosen tax bracket. See Bracket ceiling.
Run type
Which kind of test you are running: a Scenario (fixed what-if) or a Stress test (bad-luck test).
Schedule E
The tax form section where rental income and expenses are reported.
Spousal / survivor policy
The rules for what a spouse receives, such as extra Social Security, and what the surviving spouse keeps after the other dies.
Structured plan rows
Plan entries that follow a fixed layout of labeled fields, as opposed to long lists like transactions.
Survivor assumptions
The plan's guesses about what happens to income, spending and taxes after one spouse dies.
Survivor stress
A test of how the plan holds up if one spouse dies earlier than expected.
Sync actual rate
Update the plan's spending assumption to match what you actually spent.
System Configuration Console
A technical screen showing all the app's raw settings. It is meant for advanced use.
Tax bracket
A range of income taxed at one rate. As income rises, the next slice is taxed at a higher rate. Only the income inside each range is taxed at that range's rate.
Tax bracket indexing
The yearly increase of bracket limits to keep up with inflation, so rising prices alone do not push you into higher brackets.
Tax lot
One purchase of an investment on one date at one price. Buying the same stock twice creates two tax lots, each with its own cost basis.
Tax-aware sell guidance
Suggestions for which investments to sell first so that you owe the least tax.
Terminal net worth
What you are projected to own, after debts, at the end of the plan, including investments, cash, property and other assets.
Ticker
The short code for a stock or fund, such as AAPL.
Tracking Type
The top level of the spending list: income, expenses and so on. Beneath it are groups, then categories.
Trust structure
How a trust is set up: who holds the assets, who manages them, and who receives them.
Vesting
Earning full ownership over time. Stock awards from an employer often vest in stages, such as a quarter each year over four years.
